Autopilot launch — mockup v1

The missing step after onboarding: turn the settled plan into work in motion, with one confirmation and the next human touch at review. Content is superme-addon-1's real deliverables, from the docs last night's run actually wrote.

The premise: onboarding is the planning pass. Triage, manual push, "Plan it" and plan-approval all re-ask questions the interview already answered — so for this cohort they're skipped. Review never is.

3

The deputy decides whether you're needed

Human review is not reading code — it's running the thing. So the question at every review gate is: is there something the owner can exercise right now? Plumbing has nothing to demonstrate; asking you to "review" it means asking you to read a diff, which is the intensive-reading failure P0 exists to prevent.

deputy passed
CLI foundation — nothing to exercise yet
A git-access layer and repo resolution. No user-facing surface: repo-pulse <path> runs but emits nothing to look at until the digest lands. Vet green on 4 of 4 checks. Approved on your behalf — it appears in your digest, not your queue.
escalated
HTML digest — this one you should actually run
The runbook, not a diff:
repo-pulse ~/Developer/my_docs/superme open ~/.repo-pulse/superme/latest.html
You should see: one page, offline, with Today and This week.
The bar it has to clear (your words, from the PRD): "open one page offline and get the whole picture in under a minute."

The success signal finally does something. That sentence was captured at onboarding as what "good" looks like, and until now nothing consumed it. Here it IS the acceptance test — which is also why the PRD contract insists you don't paraphrase the owner's own words away.

Two guards against a rubber stamp: the deputy must name what it exercised (or state plainly that nothing was runnable), and an item whose success signal it could have run but didn't is an escalation, not a pass. It also may never drop, abandon or supersede anything — it moves work forward or sends it back, never ends it — and it may not ratify assumptions: those are calls made without you, so they keep accumulating and the close gate refuses on them. That's the human floor that survives full autopilot.

5

The close-out message

The onboarding session's last act, so it can be closed without wondering what happened.

Launched. 6 items created from 4 deliverables · 1 running now (CLI foundation) · 5 waiting on it · first review gate expected when d-cli lands.
Watch it in Pipeline. Nothing needs you until an item reaches review — I'll be here if you want to change the plan.

Three properties worth keeping: it reports what's in motion (not what was created), it says when you'll next be needed, and it names one place to look. It replaces a chat that simply stops.

Decisions for you

1 · Does the first review pause the rest?
  • Recommend no — pausing makes the launch serial and wastes the parallelism.
  • But six items arriving at review together is a big review.
  • Middle: pause after the first gate on a project's first launch only, so you calibrate trust once.
2 · Concurrency cap
  • Recommend 3 — feels parallel; a bad plan burns 3 items' tokens, not 8.
3 · Does autopilot survive review?
  • Recommend auto-continue to close — close criteria are already machine-checked, and stopping would page you twice for one item.
4 · One item per deliverable, or per wave?
  • Recommend per wave — waves are already the roadmap's unit of executable work; a deliverable can be too big for one build⟷vet loop.
